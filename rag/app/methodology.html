How this case-law search works — and how well

A plain-English note for lawyers using the ECtHR paragraph search. Last reviewed May 2026.

In one sentence. You describe a legal question; the system returns the most relevant paragraphs of Strasbourg case-law — with the exact §, the case name, and a link to HUDOC — ranked by legal substance and by how authoritative the case is.

What it searches

Every English-language ECtHR judgment in our snapshot — 19,822 cases, ~1.31 million numbered paragraphs — restricted to the substantive text (the Court's reasoning, facts, admissibility, just satisfaction and separate opinions). Pure boilerplate (headers, annexes, one-line summaries) is excluded.

How it works (without the jargon)

  • Meaning, not keywords. Each paragraph is converted into a numeric "meaning fingerprint" (an embedding). Your query is fingerprinted the same way, and the system returns the closest paragraphs. This is why it understands plain language — "can the police read my texts?" finds the Article 8 interception case-law even though those words never appear in the judgment.
  • Authority weighting. Results are nudged toward the Court's leading cases (using HUDOC's "Key case" / importance grading), because a practitioner wants the authoritative judgment, not an obscure one that happens to use similar words.
  • A second read. The top candidates are re-read against your query by a separate model to sharpen the final order.

How we measured it

We did not grade ourselves. We used the Court's own 41 official Case-Law Guides as an answer key: each Guide states a legal proposition and pinpoints the precise case paragraphs that support it. That gave us ~8,400 propositions with ~24,000 pinpoint citations. For each proposition we ask the system and check two things:

  • Right case found? (is the cited case in the top results — "docHit")
  • Right paragraph found? (is the exact cited § in the top results — "paraHit")

An independent AI-lawyer review of the returned paragraphs found ~85–90% of the top-10 genuinely relevant — higher than the "exact paragraph" figure above, because the Guides cite only some of the relevant paragraphs, so the strict measure understates real usefulness.

A notable strength: it performs as well on plain-language questions as on expert legal phrasing. Earlier keyword-based tools collapsed on lay wording; this one does not. You do not need to phrase your query like a headnote.

Where to be cautious

  • It is a research aid, not legal advice, and never a substitute for reading the judgment. Always open the case on HUDOC and verify.
  • It tells you what is relevant, not what is current law. A retrieved case may have been refined, distinguished or overtaken by later case-law. Check how the principle stands today.
  • "The paragraph" is rarely a single paragraph. The Court states a principle, applies it to the facts, and concludes across several paragraphs. The system may surface the reasoning where you expected the holding (or vice-versa). Read around the hit — click a paragraph to expand its neighbours.
  • It favours leading cases. A niche but squarely on-point lower-profile judgment can rank below a famous one.
  • Absence is not proof. If something is not in the results, that is not evidence the point does not exist — refine the query.
  • Coverage. English-language judgments in our snapshot; excludes inadmissibility-only items and non-judgment documents. The corpus is a point-in-time copy, not a live HUDOC feed.

Examples

Lay query: "can the police search my phone without a warrant?" → top results include Saber v. Norway and Kruslin / Huvig v. France — the leading Article 8 cases on search/seizure and interception.
Doctrinal query: "length of proceedings reasonable time" → the Article 6 § 1 reasonable-time line of authority (e.g. the Frydlender-type cases).
Tip: use Advanced options to restrict by respondent State or by section (e.g. "Merits" only), and to set how many cases to return.
Engine: semantic retrieval (voyage-4-large embeddings) + Court-authority weighting + neural re-ranking, over the ECtHR English corpus. Accuracy figures are measured on the Court's official Case-Law Guides and are approximate.
